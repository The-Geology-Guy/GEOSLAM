    How SLAM works

    A shallow earthquake breaks a fault. If that fault is roughly planar between the hypocentre and the surface, its trace must lie where the fault plane meets the topography. The focal mechanism gives two candidate planes (nodal planes) through the hypocentre; SLAM projects each one to the ground.

    Neither the hypocentre nor the plane orientation is known exactly, so each projected line becomes a swath: the seismo-lineament. It is a band when only the location is uncertain, and a bow tie, narrowest near the epicentre, when the orientation is uncertain too.

    The geometry

    Everything is computed in UTM metres with x east, y north and z up. A plane with dip δ and dip direction α (corrected from true to grid north) has upward unit normal

    n = (sin δ sin α, sin δ cos α, cos δ)

    For a ground point at position P relative to the hypocentre, the distance to the plane is |n·P|. The point is on the plane's uncertainty slab when that distance is no more than the half-width

    h = √( Σ (n·eᵢ)² aᵢ² )

    which is the distance to planes parallel to the fault plane and tangent to the hypocentre's uncertainty ellipsoid (semi-axes aᵢ along directions eᵢ). This is Cronin's findHalfWidth.

    Orientation uncertainty

    Cronin's code evaluates seven planes: the mean plane, the four combinations of dip ± σ and dip direction ± σ, and the steep plane at the mean direction. GEOSLAM's default instead takes every plane inside that uncertainty rectangle, which fills the region Cronin reads between the outermost lines. Switch to his seven planes under Advanced settings to reproduce published maps.

    Reading the result

    The terrain is lit from opposite the dip direction at a low sun angle, which brings out scarps facing along the fault. A mapped fault is a candidate when it lies within a seismo-lineament and its orientation and slip match that plane. SLAM narrows the search; geomorphology, seismicity and fieldwork decide.

    About GEOSLAM

    The Seismo-Lineament Analysis Method is the work of Vince Cronin (Baylor University), developed since 2004 with many students and collaborators. GEOSLAM is an open-source Python implementation by Luke Pajer, faithful to Cronin's current code and checked against published case studies such as the 2014 South Napa earthquake.

    Everything runs in your browser. Event data come from the USGS Comprehensive Catalog, terrain from USGS 3DEP, and fault traces from the USGS Quaternary Fault and Fold Database. Uploaded DEMs never leave your computer.
